1.2 Context-corrected metrics — read before any verdict below

Five corrections. Each reverses a conclusion the raw numbers support.

Reading 1 — the campaign-level pull overstates this product by roughly double, and the portfolio-level pull understates it.

Anchoring on "campaigns that contain a Hanging Closet product ad" pulls in three campaigns — Decolure_SP_Exact_PAT_Defensive, BAMBOO-KING-LIGHTGREY-6PCS_SP_Exact_PAT_Defensive and High LTSF Charge SKUS - Automatic - USA — that carry $1,501 of spend between them. Those campaigns advertise between 19 and 589 SKUs each. Hanging Closet's share of their spend is $0.45.

The check that settles it: attributing through product-ad rows gives $743.12 across both children, against $731.46 read at portfolio level. The two agree to within $11.66.

Reading 2 — the charge file's product economics predate the plan that used them.

The file carries price $41.95 and landed cost $18.86. Price moved to $39.95 around 13/14 August and SellerBoard confirms $39.95 realised; actual landed cost is $21.36. The file's margin of $7.27/unit is overstated on both sides.

The check that settles it: SellerBoard cost of goods $1,260.24 ÷ 59 units = $21.36 exactly.

Reading 3 — the 14 August plan's break-even line does not exist, and the error was inherited, not invented.

That plan priced every bid against a $5.57 break-even built from net proceeds $24.43 and landed cost $18.86 — the charge file's numbers. Correcting the plan alone would have fixed nothing, because the defect is upstream in the source file.

Reading 4 — "651 aged units" is the wrong column, and the right one is 233.

The charge file's aged-unit column counts units on hand. The column that governs the charge is Total AIS Units: 153 White + 80 Black = 233, carrying $849.89/month. Using 651 understates charge per unit by nearly two thirds.

Reading 5 — the aged surcharge is already inside the Amazon fees line.

Modelled fees $15.82/unit against $19.74 actual. The $3.92 gap is the surcharge at roughly $3.65/unit. The charge is therefore counted once, in fees, and cannot also be added back as "storage avoided". Every ceiling below is built on contribution excluding storage, then adds avoidable future charge explicitly.

3.3 Where the money goes — nowhere

This is the section that would normally reallocate. It does not, and the reason is arithmetic rather than caution.

  • The three lanes with the most reach — catch-all, broad, Black exact — are all over ceiling. Giving them more budget buys more units at a loss per unit.
  • The five lanes that clear are all at 1.3% to 5.8% utilisation. They cannot spend what they already hold, so more budget cannot reach them.

Money moved from the first group to the second would sit unspent; money left in the first group is spent above ceiling. Releasing it is the only move the data supports.

An earlier draft of this analysis recommended moving roughly $130/day into the catch-all and broad lanes on the strength of their utilisation. That recommendation is withdrawn: utilisation measures whether a lane can spend, not whether it should. Both lanes are over their own ceiling at $29.44 and $15.19.

9.3 What the analysis does not claim

  • That cutting bids will hold volume. It will not; clicks should fall roughly with the bid cut. The claim is that the units still shipped will be shipped at a price they can carry.
  • That the five clearing lanes will scale. They have one order each. Their cost per order is measured on a single conversion and is not yet a rate.
  • That Black should stop. It holds 80 charge-bearing units with 46 past 456 days. It should be cheaper, not absent.
